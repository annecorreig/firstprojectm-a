What is M&A valuation?

In a merger or acquisition (M&A), one company buys another company, or two companies combine. Before any money changes hands, both sides need to answer one question: what is this business actually worth?

Valuation is how they answer it. Analysts estimate the value of the target company using several methods. Some look at the cash the business will generate in the future. Some compare it with similar companies. Others look at what buyers paid in past deals. The result is not one "correct" number but a range of reasonable values that guides the negotiation.

Why it matters

🏢 The buyer's view

The buyer wants to pay as little as possible, and never more than the business is worth to them. That figure includes any synergies, meaning the extra savings or sales from combining the two companies. Pay too much and the buyer's shareholders lose money, even if the deal "succeeds".

🤝 The seller's view

The seller's board has a duty to get the best price for its shareholders. It wants a control premium, which is a price above today's market value, as payment for handing over control of the company. Valuation tells the seller when an offer is too low to accept.

Negotiation: The final price usually lands somewhere between the seller's minimum (its standalone value) and the buyer's maximum (standalone value plus the synergies the buyer expects). Valuation sets the edges of that zone.

Fairness opinions: Before a board approves a deal, it often hires an independent investment bank. The bank checks the valuation work and gives a fairness opinion, a letter stating that the price is fair "from a financial point of view". This protects the board and the shareholders.
