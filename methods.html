Valuation Methods

Five ways to value a company. Each answers the question "what is it worth?" from a different angle.

Key idea: enterprise value vs. equity value. Enterprise value (EV) is the value of the whole business, owed to both lenders and shareholders. Equity value is the part that belongs to shareholders only.

Equity value = Enterprise value − Net debt, where Net debt = Debt − Cash.

1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

What it is: A company is worth the cash it will produce in the future, translated into today's money. A dollar received in 5 years is worth less than a dollar today, because you could invest today's dollar and because the future is uncertain. A DCF forecasts free cash flow (cash left after running the business and investing in it), then discounts each year back to today.

When to use it: For businesses with fairly predictable cash flows. It's the core "intrinsic" method because it doesn't depend on market mood.

The discount rate: WACC

Cash flows are discounted at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), the average return that lenders and shareholders together expect. The cost of equity is usually estimated with the CAPM. Debt is cheaper because interest is tax-deductible.

Cost of equity (CAPM) = Risk-free rate + Beta × Market risk premium WACC = E/(D+E) × Cost of equity + D/(D+E) × Cost of debt × (1 − Tax rate)

Terminal value: the value after the forecast

We can't forecast forever, so after year 5 (or 10) we estimate a single terminal value for all the years beyond. It often makes up 60 to 80% of the total, so it deserves care. There are two ways to calculate it:

Gordon growth: TV = FCF(last year) × (1 + g) / (WACC − g) Exit multiple: TV = EBITDA(last year) × Exit EV/EBITDA multiple Enterprise value = Σ FCF(t) / (1 + WACC)^t + TV / (1 + WACC)^N

g = long-term growth rate (usually 2 to 3%, close to inflation). It must be lower than WACC, or the formula breaks.

✔ Pros

  • Based on the company's own fundamentals
  • Can value synergies and different scenarios explicitly
  • Doesn't depend on market mood

✘ Cons

  • Very sensitive to WACC and growth assumptions
  • Terminal value dominates the result
  • Easy to make optimistic forecasts

Worked example: Free cash flow for 3 years is $50M, $55M and $60M. WACC is 10%, g is 2%, and net debt is $150M.

PV of cash flows = 50/1.10 + 55/1.10² + 60/1.10³ = 45.5 + 45.5 + 45.1 = $136.0M

Terminal value (Gordon) = 60 × 1.02 / (0.10 − 0.02) = $765.0M → PV = 765 / 1.10³ = $574.8M

Enterprise value = 136.0 + 574.8 = $710.7M. Equity value = 710.7 − 150 = $560.7M.

Using an exit multiple instead (year-3 EBITDA of $90M × 8x = $720M, PV $541.0M) gives EV = $676.9M, so the two terminal methods roughly agree.

2. Comparable Company Analysis (trading comps)

What it is: Value the target the way the stock market values similar listed companies ("peers"). We calculate valuation multiples for the peers and apply them to the target's own figures.

When to use it: Almost always, as a market reality check. It's most useful when there are several truly similar listed companies.

EV / EBITDA → Implied EV = Target EBITDA × Peer multiple EV / Revenue → Implied EV = Target revenue × Peer multiple (for loss-making / fast growers) P / E → Implied equity = Target net income × Peer multiple

Rule: EV multiples give enterprise value. P/E gives equity value directly. Don't mix them up.

✔ Pros

  • Quick and based on real market prices
  • Easy to explain to boards and investors

✘ Cons

  • Truly comparable peers are rare
  • If the whole market is overpriced, so is your answer
  • Prices of small stock trades don't include a control premium

Worked example: TargetCo's EBITDA is $80M. Four peers trade at 8.0x, 9.5x, 10.0x and 11.5x EV/EBITDA, so the median is 9.75x.

Implied EV range: 8.0 × 80 = $640M (low), 9.75 × 80 = $780M (median), 11.5 × 80 = $920M (high).

3. Precedent Transactions

What it is: Look at what buyers actually paid for similar companies in past acquisitions, and apply those deal multiples to the target.

Why it includes a control premium: Trading comps are based on prices for buying a few shares. Precedent deals are based on prices for buying the whole company, which gives full control over strategy, cash and management. Buyers pay extra for that control, and often share some expected synergies with the seller. So precedent multiples are usually higher than trading multiples.

When to use it: In any acquisition, to see what "the going rate" for control has been in the industry.

Implied EV = Target EBITDA × Median EV/EBITDA paid in past deals

✔ Pros

  • Reflects real prices paid for control
  • Shows what the seller may expect

✘ Cons

  • Old deals happened in different markets
  • Every deal has unique synergies and circumstances
  • Data on private deals is often limited

Worked example: Three recent deals in the industry were priced at 10x, 11x and 13x EBITDA. Using the median of 11x: 11 × $80M = $880M. That's about 13% above the trading-comps median of $780M. The difference is roughly the control premium.

4. Leveraged Buyout (LBO) analysis

What it is (simplified): An LBO asks: "What is the most a private equity firm could pay and still earn its target return?" The firm buys the company using mostly borrowed money, pays the debt down with the company's cash flow, and sells after about 5 years. Because the debt shrinks, the owners' share of the company grows.

When to use it: When private equity firms might be competing bidders. The LBO value acts as a "floor" price that a financial buyer could pay.

Equity at exit = Exit EV − Remaining debt Money multiple = Equity at exit / Equity invested IRR = Money multiple^(1 / years) − 1

✔ Pros

  • Shows what a financial buyer can afford
  • Makes the effect of debt clear

✘ Cons

  • Ignores strategic synergies, so it often gives a lower value
  • Depends on how much lenders will lend

Worked example: A PE firm buys TargetCo for 8x EBITDA of $80M = $640M, funded with $384M of debt (60%) and $256M of equity (40%). After 5 years, EBITDA is $100M and it sells at 8x = $800M. Debt has been paid down to $200M.

Equity at exit = 800 − 200 = $600M. Money multiple = 600 / 256 = 2.34x. IRR = 2.34^(1/5) − 1 ≈ 18.6% a year. If the firm needs 20%, it would have to pay a bit less than $640M.

5. Asset-based valuation

What it is: Value the company as the fair market value of everything it owns, minus everything it owes. The book values on the balance sheet are adjusted to realistic market values, for example property bought 20 years ago is revalued to today's price.

When to use it: For asset-heavy businesses (real estate, shipping, banks, holding companies), for companies in distress, or as a "floor" value in liquidation.

Net asset value = Fair value of assets − Fair value of liabilities

✔ Pros

  • Based on tangible, checkable items
  • Good minimum value ("floor")

✘ Cons

  • Misses intangibles: brand, customers, know-how
  • Undervalues healthy, growing companies

Worked example: TargetCo's assets have a book value of $750M. Revaluing its land and factories raises this to a fair value of $900M. Liabilities are $500M. Net asset value = 900 − 500 = $400M of equity value. That's much lower than the other methods, because it ignores TargetCo's customer relationships and earning power.

Evaluating the process: where valuations go wrong

Research has repeatedly found that many acquisitions destroy value for the buyer's shareholders, while the seller's shareholders usually do well. This is rarely because of maths errors. It's usually because of human biases:

📈 Optimistic projections

Management teams and advisers are paid when deals happen, so forecasts tend to drift upward. Each small optimistic assumption (growth, margins, timing) compounds into a much bigger value. Remedy: compare forecasts with the company's actual history and run a downside case.

🤝 Overpaying for synergies

If the buyer pays a premium equal to all expected synergies, the seller captures all the value, and the buyer carries all the risk that synergies never appear. Remedy: discount synergies by their probability, subtract integration costs, and only give away part of them in the premium.

🏆 The winner's curse

In an auction, bidders' estimates are spread out around the true value. The winner is often the bidder who overestimated the most, so "winning" often means overpaying. Remedy: set a walk-away price before bidding starts, and stick to it.

⚓ Anchoring and confirmation bias

Once a number is mentioned (for example the seller's asking price), people adjust insufficiently away from it, and they look for evidence that supports the deal they want. Remedy: ask an independent team to challenge the case for the deal.

Why use several methods together? Each method has a different blind spot. DCF depends on your assumptions, comps depend on market mood, precedents depend on past conditions, the LBO ignores synergies, and asset-based valuation ignores intangibles. If several independent methods land in a similar range, you can trust that range more. If they disagree, the gap tells you what to investigate. That's the purpose of the football field chart.
