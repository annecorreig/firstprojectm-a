Accretion
When a deal increases the buyer's earnings per share (EPS). This happens when the target's earnings are larger than the cost of paying for it (interest on new debt plus the effect of new shares).
Asset-based valuation
Valuing a company as the fair value of its assets minus its liabilities. Often used as a minimum ("floor") value.
Beta (β)
A measure of how much a stock moves compared with the overall market. A beta of 1.2 means it tends to move 20% more than the market, so it is riskier and investors expect a higher return.
CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model)
A formula to estimate the cost of equity: risk-free rate + beta × market risk premium.
Capital expenditure (Capex)
Money spent on long-term assets such as machinery, buildings or IT systems. It is subtracted when calculating free cash flow.
Closing
The moment the deal legally completes: the money and shares change hands and the buyer takes ownership.
Comparable company analysis (trading comps)
Valuing a company using the valuation multiples of similar listed companies.
Control premium
The extra amount above the current market price that a buyer pays to gain control of a company. It is typically 20 to 40% for public companies.
Cost of debt
The interest rate a company pays on its borrowing. Because interest is tax-deductible, the after-tax cost is cost of debt × (1 − tax rate).
Cost of equity
The return shareholders expect for the risk of owning the company's shares. It is usually estimated with the CAPM.
Discounted cash flow (DCF)
A valuation method that forecasts future free cash flows and converts them into today's value using a discount rate (usually WACC).
Dilution
When a deal reduces the buyer's earnings per share. It is common when many new shares are issued or the target earns little relative to the price.
Due diligence
The detailed investigation of a target's finances, contracts, legal issues, customers and operations before signing a deal.
Earn-out
Part of the price that is paid later, only if the acquired business reaches agreed targets. It helps bridge disagreements about the forecasts.
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation and Amortization. A rough measure of operating profit before financing and accounting choices, widely used in multiples.
Enterprise value (EV)
The value of the entire business, belonging to both lenders and shareholders. EV = equity value + net debt.
EPS (Earnings per share)
Net income divided by the number of shares outstanding.
Equity value
The value belonging to shareholders only. Equity value = enterprise value − net debt. For a listed company, this is its market capitalization.
Exit multiple
A valuation multiple (often EV/EBITDA) used to estimate the terminal value, or the sale price of a company in an LBO.
Fairness opinion
A letter from an independent financial adviser stating that a deal's price is fair, from a financial point of view, to the shareholders.
Football field chart
A horizontal bar chart showing the valuation range from each method side by side, used to settle on an overall range.
Free cash flow (FCF)
Cash generated by the business after operating costs, taxes, capex and working-capital needs. It is the cash available to pay lenders and shareholders.
Gordon growth model
A way to calculate terminal value that assumes cash flows grow at a constant rate forever: FCF × (1 + g) / (WACC − g).
Integration costs
One-off costs to combine two companies, such as severance, IT systems and rebranding. They reduce the net value of synergies.
IRR (Internal rate of return)
The annual return an investment earns. Private equity firms often target an IRR of 20% or more.
Leveraged buyout (LBO)
Buying a company using a large amount of borrowed money, which is repaid from the company's own cash flow. Typically done by private equity firms.
Market risk premium
The extra return investors expect from the stock market as a whole over a risk-free investment. It is often assumed to be 4 to 6%.
Multiple (valuation multiple)
A ratio that compares value with a financial figure, for example EV/EBITDA = 10x means the business is valued at 10 times its EBITDA.
Net debt
Total debt minus cash. It is subtracted from enterprise value to get equity value.
Normalized EBITDA
EBITDA adjusted to remove one-off or unusual items, so it shows the business's ongoing earning power.
P/E ratio (Price / Earnings)
Share price divided by earnings per share. It values equity directly rather than the whole enterprise.
Precedent transactions
Valuing a company using the multiples paid in past acquisitions of similar companies. These include a control premium.
Risk-free rate
The return on an investment with almost no risk of default, usually the yield on long-term government bonds.
Synergies
Extra value created by combining two companies. Cost synergies are savings, and revenue synergies are extra sales. Cost synergies are usually more reliable.
Terminal value (TV)
The value of all cash flows after the explicit forecast period in a DCF. It is calculated with the Gordon growth model or an exit multiple.
WACC (Weighted average cost of capital)
The blended return expected by all of a company's investors (lenders and shareholders), weighted by how much each contributes. It is used as the discount rate in a DCF.
Winner's curse
In an auction, the winning bidder is often the one who overestimated the value the most, so winning tends to mean overpaying.
