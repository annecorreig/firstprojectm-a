The Valuation Process

How a buyer gets from "we should buy that company" to a signed deal, in eight steps.

Running example: BuyerCo, a packaging company, is thinking about acquiring TargetCo, a smaller competitor. All numbers are made up and in millions of dollars ($M).

  1. 1

    Define the deal objective and strategic rationale

    Before running any numbers, the buyer writes down why it wants this company. Possible reasons include entering a new market, buying technology, gaining customers, or cutting costs by combining operations. The rationale decides which synergies are realistic and which valuation methods matter most.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Starting with "the target is cheap" instead of a clear strategic fit. Doing deals because competitors are doing them, or because management wants a bigger company ("empire building").

    Example: BuyerCo wants TargetCo's 40 regional customers and expects to close 2 overlapping factories. Its objective: "Grow regional market share from 12% to 18% and lower unit costs."

  2. 2

    Gather and analyze financial information

    This is due diligence: a detailed investigation of the target's accounts, contracts, customers, debts, legal risks and management. A key task is normalizing EBITDA. Analysts remove one-off items, such as a lawsuit settlement or a factory closure, so the earnings reflect what the business normally makes.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Accepting the seller's "adjusted EBITDA" without checking it. Sellers like to label too many costs as "one-off". Another mistake is skipping working-capital and debt-like items such as unpaid pensions.

    Example: TargetCo reports EBITDA of $70M. Diligence finds a $12M one-off legal charge (added back) and $2M of owner's personal expenses (added back), but also a $4M rent discount ending next year (deducted). Normalized EBITDA = 70 + 12 + 2 − 4 = $80M.

  3. 3

    Build financial projections

    Analysts forecast revenue, costs, investment (capital expenditure, or capex) and free cash flow for the next 5 to 10 years. Projections should be tied to clear assumptions, such as market growth, pricing and margins, and checked against the company's past results.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    "Hockey-stick" forecasts, where growth suddenly speeds up right after the deal. Margins that improve every year with no explanation. Forgetting that growth needs extra investment in equipment and working capital.

    Example: TargetCo's revenue grew 4% a year historically. The base case assumes 5% growth and free cash flow rising from $100M to $140M over 5 years. A downside case uses 2% growth, and an upside case uses 8%.

  4. 4

    Apply multiple valuation methods

    No single method is reliable on its own. Analysts typically run a DCF, trading comparables, precedent transactions and sometimes an LBO or asset-based valuation. Each method shows the company from a different angle.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Choosing peer companies that aren't really similar. Using a discount rate that doesn't match the target's risk. Mixing up enterprise value and equity value, for example by applying an EV multiple and forgetting to subtract debt.

    Example: DCF gives TargetCo an enterprise value of $950M to $1,250M. Trading comps (8x to 11.5x EBITDA of $80M) give $640M to $920M. Precedent deals (10x to 13x) give $800M to $1,040M.

  5. 5

    Estimate synergies and integration costs

    Synergies are the extra value created by combining the companies. Cost synergies come from savings, such as closing duplicate offices or buying in bulk, and are usually more reliable. Revenue synergies come from selling more, for example cross-selling to each other's customers, and are much less certain. Getting synergies costs money up front, through severance, IT migration and rebranding. These are the integration costs.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Counting revenue synergies at full value. Assuming savings arrive on day one instead of over 2 to 3 years. Leaving out integration costs and the risk that customers or key staff leave.

    Example: Closing 2 factories saves $30M a year (cost synergy), and cross-selling may add $10M a year of profit (revenue synergy). The present value of all synergies is about $400M, but BuyerCo only expects to achieve 75% of that, or $300M. One-off integration costs are $60M.

  6. 6

    Triangulate a valuation range (football field chart)

    The results of all methods are put side by side in a football field chart: a horizontal bar for each method showing its low-to-high range. Where the bars overlap is the most defensible value range. You can build one yourself in the football field calculator.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Simply averaging every method, even when one is clearly less relevant. Making ranges so wide they say nothing. Only showing the methods that support the price you already want.

    Example: Most of TargetCo's bars (DCF, precedents and LBO) overlap between about $950M and $1,050M, so BuyerCo's team picks a standalone value of about $1,000M as the midpoint.

  7. 7

    Determine the offer price, control premium, and deal structure

    The buyer sets an opening offer and a walk-away price. Public targets usually require a control premium of roughly 20 to 40% over the current share price. The buyer also chooses the deal structure. Paying in cash gives sellers certainty, and the buyer keeps all the upside. Paying in stock shares the risk (and the synergies) with the seller's shareholders. The accretion/dilution test checks how the deal affects the buyer's earnings per share.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    Paying a premium larger than the expected net synergies, which means the buyer gives away more than the deal creates. Treating "EPS accretive" as proof that a deal creates value. Ignoring how the payment method changes the risk.

    Example: Standalone value $1,000M + expected synergies $300M − integration costs $60M = walk-away price of $1,240M. BuyerCo offers a 20% premium ($1,200M), paid 50% in cash and 50% in BuyerCo shares.

  8. 8

    Negotiate, fairness opinion, and closing

    The two sides negotiate price, payment method, protections such as an earn-out (extra payment only if targets are hit), and break-up fees. The target's board usually gets an independent fairness opinion. Then shareholders and regulators approve the deal, the money and shares change hands at closing, and integration begins.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    "Deal fever": raising the bid again and again in a competitive auction to avoid losing. Treating the fairness opinion as a formality. Not planning integration until after closing.

    Example: After two rounds, the parties agree on $1,180M plus a $40M earn-out if TargetCo's EBITDA reaches $95M within 2 years. The fairness opinion confirms the price is within the valuation range, and the deal closes 4 months later.
